## Concepts and definitions

### What a policy marker is

A policy marker is a classification applied to individual bilateral ODA activities reported in the OECD DAC Creditor Reporting System (CRS). It records whether a project targets a specific cross-cutting policy objective — such as gender equality or climate change — and to what degree.

Policy markers do not create a separate database. They are a layer of classification on top of CRS activities. Each activity can carry multiple markers simultaneously (for example, a project can be marked for both gender equality and climate adaptation).

### What the markers cover

The CRS uses two groups of markers: policy markers and Rio markers. Together they are often referred to collectively as "policy markers." The distinction matters for analysis:

- Policy markers track cross-cutting social and governance objectives.
- Rio markers track environmental objectives linked to the 1992 Rio Conventions.

### Full list of markers

The table below lists all current markers, their group, and the year they were introduced for CRS reporting.

| **Marker** | **Group** | **Introduced** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Environment | Policy marker | 1992 |
| Gender equality | Policy marker | 1997 (data from 2002 for commitments; 2009 for disbursements) |
| Aid to trade | Policy marker | 2007 |
| Disaster risk reduction (DRR) / Sendai Framework | Policy marker | 2016 |
| Nutrition | Policy marker | 2016 |
| Inclusion and empowerment of persons with disabilities | Policy marker | 2016 |
| Reproductive, maternal, newborn and child health (RMNCH) | Policy marker | 2016 |
| Human rights-based approach (HRBA) | Policy marker | 2016 |
| Climate change mitigation | Rio marker | 1998 |
| Biodiversity | Rio marker | 1998 |
| Desertification | Rio marker | 1998 |
| Climate change adaptation | Rio marker | 2010 |

Note on the environment marker: The environment marker is a composite. It takes the highest value recorded across the three original Rio markers (biodiversity, climate change mitigation, desertification). It is rarely used in isolation for analysis; use the individual Rio markers instead.

### What the markers can and cannot tell you

Can tell you:

- Whether a donor intended a project to address a given policy objective.
- Trends in donor prioritisation of a policy area over time.
- Shares of bilateral allocable ODA targeting a given objective.

Cannot tell you:

- How much money was actually spent on the policy objective within a project (the whole project value is counted, not just the relevant component).
- Whether the project achieved its stated objective.
- Anything about core contributions to multilateral organisations (these are not marked by DAC donors individually; multilaterals report separately).

### Coverage: what is and is not included

Policy markers apply only to bilateral allocable ODA. The following are excluded:

- General budget support
- Core contributions to multilateral organisations
- Imputed student costs
- Debt relief
- Administrative costs
- Development awareness
- In-country refugee costs

Earmarked funding to multilateral organisations (reported as bilateral aid under OECD rules) is included.

## How to approach it

### Deciding whether to suggest policy marker analysis

Suggest policy marker analysis when the user's question is about:

- How much ODA a donor directs toward a specific cross-cutting objective (gender, climate, nutrition, etc.).
- How a donor's prioritisation of a policy area has changed over time.
- Comparing donors' policy commitments across a shared objective.

Do not suggest policy marker analysis when the user's question is about:

- Sector-level spending (use CRS purpose codes instead).
- Total ODA volumes with no reference to a specific policy objective.
- Non-DAC providers (policy markers only apply to DAC members).

### Caveats to flag in any analysis

Always note these limitations:

1. Self-reported and subjective. Donors apply markers themselves. Reporting practices differ across donors, which limits strict comparability.
2. Partial screening. Not all activities are screened against all markers. "Not screened" (never assessed) does not mean "not targeted" (assessed and found not to qualify). See the policy-markers skill for the full distinction.
3. Whole-project counting. The full project value is counted, not just the share attributable to the policy objective.

## Pitfalls

Treating "not screened" as "not targeted." These are different values with different meanings. A large share of "not screened" activities in a portfolio may indicate incomplete reporting, not absence of policy intent. See the policy-markers skill for the full distinction.

Adding Rio marker totals without checking for overlap. A single activity can be marked for both climate mitigation and adaptation. Adding the two totals produces double counting. Use the microdataset to identify and handle overlap.

Using the environment marker as a proxy for climate. The environment marker is a composite of three Rio markers and is not equivalent to climate-related ODA. Use the climate mitigation and adaptation markers directly.

## What a good answer looks like

1. Confirm which marker applies and what it covers.
2. State the coverage (bilateral allocable ODA only) and the time period available.
3. Note whether the analysis uses commitments or disbursements and why (see the policy-markers skill for when to use commitments vs. disbursements).
4. Flag the self-reporting caveat and any known issues with the specific marker.
5. If the user asks about totals combining principal and significant, label this clearly as "gender-related" or "climate-related" ODA (not "gender ODA" or "climate ODA"), to reflect that the marker captures intent, not outcome.
